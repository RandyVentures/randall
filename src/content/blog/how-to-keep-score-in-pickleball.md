Pickleball scoring is the part of the game everyone pretends to understand. Three numbers, a score that only goes up for one side, and a "second server" who is somehow the first person to serve. Ten minutes into your first game, someone asks "wait, what's the score?" and nobody is sure.

It's simpler than it sounds once you see the logic. This guide covers traditional side-out scoring, which is what most rec play and most sanctioned events use, then walks through a real sequence rally by rally.

## The basic rules

- **Only the serving side scores.** If you're receiving and you win the rally, you don't get a point. You get the serve.
- **Games go to 11, win by 2.** A game at 11-10 keeps going until someone leads by two. Some events play to 15 or 21.
- **The server calls the score out loud before every serve.** This is a rule, not just good manners, and it's the best defense against losing track.
- **The same server keeps serving until their side loses a rally.** Each time the serving side wins a point, the server switches sides with their partner (in doubles) and serves again.

## Doubles: the three-number score

In doubles the score has three parts, called in this order:

1. **The serving team's score**
2. **The receiving team's score**
3. **The server number**, 1 or 2

So **5-3-2** means the serving team has 5, the receivers have 3, and this is the team's second server.

Each team gets two servers per turn. When server 1 loses a rally, their partner becomes server 2 and serves from wherever they are standing. When server 2 loses a rally, that's a **side out**: the serve goes to the other team, and their player on the right side of the court starts as server 1.

## Why every game starts at 0-0-2

Serving first is an advantage, so the first team only gets one server on its opening turn. Calling the first serve **0-0-2** marks that player as the second server. When they lose a rally, it's a side out right away.

This is the call that confuses most new players. The "2" isn't about who is serving. It just means "this is the last server before the serve changes hands."

## Where to stand: even and odd

Your team's own score tells you which side the server serves from.

- **Singles:** serve from the right when your score is even (0, 2, 4…), from the left when it's odd.
- **Doubles:** the player who **started the game on the right** is back on the right whenever the team's score is even, and on the left when it's odd.

This doubles rule is the best way to catch a mistake. If your team has 6 and the player who started on the right is standing on the left, something has gone wrong. Partners only switch sides after their team wins a point on serve. The receiving team never switches.

## A rally-by-rally example

Team A (Ana and Ben) serves first. Team B (Cruz and Dee) receives. Ana and Cruz start on the right side of their courts.

| What happened | Score called before the next serve | Who serves, from where |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Game starts | **0-0-2** | Ana, from the right |
| Ana's team wins the rally | **1-0-2** | Ana and Ben switch sides. Ana serves from the left (odd score). |
| Ana's team loses the rally | Side out → **0-1-1** | Team B's player on the right, Cruz, serves. Team B's score is called first now. |
| Cruz's team wins the rally | **1-1-1** | Cruz and Dee switch. Cruz serves from the left. |
| Cruz's team loses the rally | **1-1-2** | Dee becomes server 2 and serves from where Dee is standing, the right. |
| Dee's team loses the rally | Side out → **1-1-1** | Back to Team A. Ben is on the right side now, so Ben serves first. |

Notice the score flips around on a side out. The numbers stay with the teams, but the serving team's score is always called first.

## Singles scoring

Singles drops the server number. The score is just **server's score, receiver's score**, like 4-2. There's no 0-0-2 start, and there's no second server. Lose the rally on your serve and it's a side out.

## Rally scoring

Some leagues, events, and pro formats use **rally scoring**, where a point is scored on every rally no matter who served. Games are usually longer, to 15 or 21. If you're joining a league, ask which format it uses before the first game. The rest of this guide is about traditional side-out scoring.

## How to stop losing track

- **Call the full score every serve, loudly.** Ask your opponents to repeat it back if there's any doubt.
- **Use the even/odd check.** If the score and the server's position don't agree, stop and fix it before the serve.
- **Rebuild from the last agreed score.** When everyone remembers it differently, go back to the last call nobody disputed and replay what happened since.
- **Let something else hold the score.** A clip-on flip counter, a court scoreboard, or a phone or watch app means nobody has to keep three numbers in their head during a long rally.
