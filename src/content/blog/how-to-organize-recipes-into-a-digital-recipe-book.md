Everyone's recipe collection looks about the same: 80 screenshots, a few dozen bookmarks, saved TikToks and Instagram posts, a stained index card in a drawer, and one recipe you only know because your mom texted it to you in 2019.

Here's how to turn all of that into a digital recipe book you'll actually cook from. It's timely, too. Thanksgiving is next week, and this is the week everyone goes hunting for "the stuffing recipe."

## 1. Pick one home

This is the step that matters most. A notes app, a shared Google Doc, a spreadsheet, or a recipe app can all work. What doesn't work is three of them. If you have to remember where a recipe lives, you'll end up searching the internet for it again.

What to look for in that home:

- It can **import from links** so you're not retyping.
- It **works offline** in the kitchen.
- It **searches** by name and ingredient.
- It **syncs** to your other devices, or is easy to share with family.

## 2. Gather everything first

Before you organize anything, collect it all in one pass:

- Phone screenshots and photo albums
- Browser bookmarks
- Saved posts on TikTok, Instagram, Pinterest, and YouTube
- Emails and texts with recipes in them
- Recipe cards, printouts, and the cookbooks with sticky notes

## 3. Keep only what you'll make

Be honest. Keep recipes you've made and liked, and ones you'll make in the next few months. A hundred recipes you use beats a thousand you scroll past.

## 4. Use the same format for every recipe

Each recipe gets the same parts, in the same order:

1. **Title**
2. **Servings and total time**
3. **Ingredients**, with amounts
4. **Steps**, numbered
5. **Source**, the link or the person
6. **Your notes**

Social posts and screenshots often leave out amounts or bury them in a caption. Copy the real ingredient list into the recipe, and keep the link as the source.

## 5. A few categories, lots of tags

Keep top-level categories few and obvious: **breakfast, mains, sides, soups, baking, desserts, drinks.** Then use tags for everything else: *weeknight, holiday, freezer-friendly, kid favorite, Grandma's.* Tags let one recipe live in several places without duplicates.

## 6. Digitize the family recipe cards

These are the recipes worth the most effort, and the ones most at risk.

- **Photograph each card** flat in daylight, without flash. Get the back too.
- **Type or scan the text** into your recipe book.
- **Keep the photo of the original** next to the typed version. The handwriting is part of the recipe.
- **Write down whose recipe it was.**
- **Translate the vague parts** while someone can still answer: "a moderate oven," "butter the size of an egg," "cook until done."

If the card is in old cursive that's hard to read, I wrote a guide to [reading old handwritten cursive](/blog/how-to-read-old-cursive-letters/).

## 7. Add notes every time you cook

The best recipe books have margins full of notes: *"use less salt," "double the sauce," "bake 5 minutes longer in our oven."* A digital book should have them too. Add a note the day you cook, while you remember.
