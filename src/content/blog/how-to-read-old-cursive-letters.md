A box of old family letters is a strange thing to inherit. You can tell they matter. You can make out "Dear" and a name, and then the cursive turns into loops you can't read.

The good news: old handwriting is learnable, and AI has gotten much better at it. The catch is knowing what each tool is good at, and where it will quietly get things wrong.

## First, a warning about "cursive translators"

Search for a cursive translator and most of the top results are **cursive font generators**. You type text and they give you fancy cursive letters back. That's the opposite of what you need for an old letter.

What you want is **handwriting recognition** (sometimes called handwritten text recognition, or cursive to text). You give it a photo of the page, and it gives you typed text.

## How to read old cursive yourself

Even if you use AI, these steps are how you check its work.

### 1. Start with what you can read

Names, "Dear," "love," "the," "and," and dates are usually readable first. Write them down. Each one gives you real examples of this writer's letters.

### 2. Build the writer's alphabet

Make a small chart: for each letter you've confirmed, copy how this person writes it. Every hand is different, so a chart from the letter itself beats any guide. Capitals and the letters r, s, e, and t are where writers vary most.

### 3. Decode the hard words letter by letter

Use your chart to sound out words one letter at a time. Count the humps in m, n, u, and w, because those are the easiest letters to misread. If a word still won't come, mark it [?] and keep going. Context from later in the letter often solves it.

### 4. Know the old letterforms and abbreviations

- **The long s.** In older writing an s in the middle of a word can look like an f without the crossbar: "pleaſe" is please.
- **Doubled letters** were sometimes written as one letter with a line over it.
- **Superscript endings:** "yr" for your, "wd" for would, "Wm" for William, "Jas" for James.
- **Dates:** "inst." meant this month, and "ult." meant last month. "The 4th inst." is the 4th of the current month.
- **"&c"** is etc.

### 5. Read the whole thing for meaning

Spelling in old letters is often phonetic, and punctuation is scarce. Read it out loud. A sentence that makes no sense on paper often makes sense when you hear it.

## Take a good photo first

The photo matters more than the tool you use.

- **Use soft daylight**, near a window, not direct sun.
- **Turn the flash off.** Flash creates glare on old paper and ink.
- **Lay the page flat** and hold the phone parallel to it.
- **Fill the frame** with the whole page, edges included.
- **Photograph both sides**, and each page separately.
- **Handle carefully.** Clean, dry hands, and don't force a folded page flat if it resists.

## Can AI read cursive?

Yes, a lot of it. Modern AI models can turn a clear photo of a handwritten letter into text in seconds, including writing a hundred years old. A few honest limits:

- **Names, places and dates are the weak spot.** These are the words you care about most, and AI can't use context to guess a great-aunt's maiden name or a small town.
- **It can smooth things over.** AI may "fix" spelling or skip a word it can't read instead of flagging it.
- **Bad photos and faded ink** still lower the accuracy a lot.

The best way to use it is as a fast first draft. Then compare the text to the original page, line by line, and correct it.
